Article No. 006 · Book Review Division (est. today, under protest)

The Wealth of Wyrms

The Journal of Jacobson Studies Book Reviews Vol. I · pp. 1147–1153

Review Essay

The Wealth of Wyrms: On E. E. Knight’s Dragon Champion

Knight, E. E. Dragon Champion (The Age of Fire, Book One). Twenty-six chapters, an epilogue, and a five-term glossary of Drakine. Reviewed from an uncorrected electronic proof, ver. 0.7, whose own front matter warns “there are some errors you are warned.” There are. I was.

Reviewed by Paul Jacobson, Dr.

Department of Economics; colossus of economic thought (by any objective measure, particularly his own)

Abstract—Assigned, over the reviewer’s formal objection, a 300-page novel about a dragon, the reviewer set out to demolish the claim that occasioned the assignment and reports instead the most alarming finding of his career: the claim survives. Dragon Champion is a growth-and-institutions monograph in a fireproof jacket — it endogenizes the dragon hoard, states the permanent income hypothesis in dwarvish verse, anticipates endogenous growth theory in a senile black dragon’s library, and equips its villain with a rank-order tournament scheme and a recruitment model of extremism that several journals in my field would print with minor revisions. Deficiencies remain: the credit market is missing, the dragon-hunting industry operates at a visible loss, and the proof was typeset by an enemy. Accept.

JEL codes: D23, L14, N40, O31, Q32, Z13. Drakine: foua, prrum.

Editorial note. The reviewer moved that this assignment be desk-rejected on the grounds that the Journal does not review novels, and certainly not novels whose cover depicts the author of the assignment’s difficulties. The Editor-in-Chief overruled him. The reviewer appealed. The appeal was heard, with visible enjoyment, by the Editor-in-Chief. All three officials are me. The motion failed, 1–2.

I. Provenance of the Assignment

A first-year in my ECON 101 section — name withheld pending the grade appeal they will lose — requested extra credit for having read this book over break, on the stated grounds that it covers “basically the same material as the course, but the economy has dragons in it.” I accepted the submission the way one accepts a duel. I read all twenty-six chapters, the epilogue, and the glossary in a spirit of pedagogical vengeance, red pen loaded, intending to return the novel and the claim in matching condition.

I report the result in the interest of scientific transparency, which is to say through gritted teeth: the student’s thesis survives peer review. This has never happened before in the history of this Journal, and measures are being drafted to ensure it does not become habitual.

II. The Work, Summarized for Colleagues

The study follows a single economic agent, Auron, a gray dragon hatched with two endowment shocks: no scales, and — the crucial one — no appetite for gold. Orphaned by a joint elvish–dwarvish extraction venture, the subject proceeds through a career of remarkable sectoral breadth: subsistence forager; merged partner in a wolf-pack (Ch. 10, the Coasean high point of modern letters); armored-car service for a dwarvish trading company; graduate study under the last literate dragon; feudal lord supplying defense and courts to a blighter principality; and finally saboteur inside a command economy that breeds dragons for race war. He ends the book married, propertied, and drafting early-childhood policy. The student was right. It is the syllabus. The economy has dragons in it.

III. The Central Contribution: The Hoard, Endogenized

Begin with the result that justifies the volume. Fantasy literature has slept for a century on what I will call the Smaug Problem: why does a dragon — a creature with no shops, no heirs, and no observed transactions — sit on a pile of currency?1 The standard treatment is psychiatric. Knight’s treatment is economic, and it is delivered in one line of dialogue that I have now read aloud to three colleagues without invitation:

“Scaled dragons eat the metal. It gets turned into armor. Since they shed them sometimes, a dragon will hoard money so his coat stays healthy.” — Auron, to the tradesdwarf Djer (proof, Ch. 12)

The hoard is not a pathology. It is inventory. Gold is an intermediate input to scale production — health capital with a shine — and the demand for treasure is therefore a derived demand, complete with a stated depreciation channel (“they shed them”). Auron’s own father waves off his modest trove with “I’d rather eat the gold than keep it to look at,” the least sentimental sentence about wealth in the whole of dragon letters. In one stroke the author converts an entire genre’s decorative miserliness into a theory of the firm. Tolkien gave us a dragon sleeping on his wealth — a zero-velocity benchmark, useful only for calibration. Knight gives us a balance sheet.

The result is then used, which is what separates scholarship from ornament. The gray dragon, lacking scales, lacks the appetite; the Chartered Company of the Diadem accordingly hires him to guard its caravan’s expense wagon, replacing mercenaries at — the text is precise — not one-tenth their cost, “perhaps not even one one-hundredth.” The dwarf Djer supplies the theorem: “Funny how trustworthiness costs more than muscle.” It is the only novel known to this office in which a character is hired specifically for the shape of his utility function, and the arrangement is incentive-compatible on its face: the guard cannot be bribed with the cargo because the cargo does not enter his preferences. My own department has never solved its committee-staffing problem this cleanly.2

IV. Growth Theory in a Dark Cave

The novel’s second sustained contribution belongs to the growth literature, which is to say it belongs to me. NooMoahk, an ancient black dragon losing his memory in a ruined blighter city, keeps no gold. He keeps a library, and he explains why with a clarity my doctoral cohort did not reach until the second year:

“Treasure, as you understand it, Auron, is a dead thing. It doesn’t know how to make more of itself. This is knowledge… The more of it that’s in your head, the more your head can hold. It breeds on its own.” — NooMoahk the Black (proof, Ch. 16)

Non-rivalry; increasing returns; the complementarity of ideas (“you never know what the next bit of reading is going to… meet up with in your head and mate”). The dragon adds the market test: sages once crossed a landmass to consult him and paid in chests of money, because “anyone could mine and make money; no money could buy the making of liquid fire… if the knowledge was lost.” A creature the book repeatedly calls senile has grasped, in a cave, what several national governments did not grasp until the latter twentieth century, and what I later documented at definitive length in The Miracle on the Han River (see the Dissertation, Chs. I–V; the resemblance flatters the dragon).3 His dementia, note, is not wasted pathos: it is depreciation of knowledge capital in an economy with exactly one archive and no succession plan. The library survives him only because the protagonist studied in it. Endowments, colleagues, are also a growth policy.

The dwarves, meanwhile, teach intertemporal choice to their children in verse. Their founding parable — sons who inherit a Golden Tree that drops nuggets seasonally, and who chop it down for the trunk — ends: “Money that came quick was spent quick, and their great-grandchildren knew poverty.” That is the resource curse and the permanent income hypothesis, rhymed, for an audience of dwarf children. I present the same material every autumn to university students, unrhymed, with lower retention. And the book’s quietest large claim is put in the mouth of a dwarf philosopher-king: the long-lived “greater” races stand alone and magnificent, while the short-lived ones “stand on the shoulders of the last generation. In time, the pyramids of the Lesser will be the taller.” Growth is not longevity. Growth is transmission. The elves of this book never learn it; the reader is invited to inspect the elves of this book.

V. Field Notes on Institutions

A partial inventory, offered so the reader understands the reviewer’s deteriorating position as he attempted to dislike this novel:

  1. The Chartered Company of the Diadem. A dwarvish joint-stock partnership with a written charter, contested quorum rules (a partnership is created before the protagonist’s eyes via “a Simple Majority of the Founding Partners… Paragraph Two of Article Nine”), buy-ins financed by abstinence from tobacco and beer, and an origin story in pure transport-cost arbitrage: ten porters carrying cargo past a waterfall. The firm audits its treasury by dusting the coin with poison — a monitoring technology — and when an embezzlement occurs anyway, it proceeds through a hollowed walking stick, confirming the oldest result in public finance: expense accounts leak at the joints.4
  2. Allocation of control rights. An elvish river captain, quarreling with a Partner: “It’s my ship from when it leaves the falls until we touch sand at Wallander. Then it’s the Company’s ship again.” The charter-party stated as a boundary-of-the-firm problem, by a character with cattails in his hair.
  3. The Suerzain of Wa’ah, “a monarch with the wisdom to leave a good thing alone by not levying tolls, duties, dock fees, or taxes” on the entrepôt trade, who instead competes in the market with well-sited warehouses. The caravan returns home “with three times the goods and one-tenth the money” — the gains from trade reported as cheerfully as a weather observation. Contrast the toll-takers upriver, of whom a dwarf remarks that they can “count their coin and rot.” The author has views on tariffs. They are correct.
  4. The wolf merger. Cornered by a pack and having killed most of it, the drake proposes, in pidgin: “Why we two fight?… We make pack. Pack has two.” A negative-sum conflict is converted by bargain into a two-member firm with an agreed governance structure (“You leader”), later ratified by a thousand-wolf assembly — the Thing — which itself cannot convene without stripping the game from three river valleys. Congestion costs of the legislature: observed, priced, and adjourned.
  5. The feudal contract, drafted aloud. Auron’s bargain with the blighters specifies territory, exclusivity, protection services, famine insurance (“I will succor the Umazheh as I can by hunting”), and, in time, appellate jurisdiction over watering rights and orphan custody. His stewardship platform is sustainable-yield management — his father’s “overhunt a forest one year, and you will starve the next” is the whole of renewable-resource economics in eleven words — and when a war prophet comes recruiting, the chieftain Unrush is argued out of it with an opportunity-cost accounting I intend to set as an exam question: “Your stonemasons will have to swing axes rather than hammers, your blacksmiths will make weapons instead of tools, and your laborers will carry spears rather than earth.” Guns and butter, for readers who believe economists invented the frontier.

VI. The Political Economy of the Villain

The Wyrmmaster of the Isle of Ice, who breeds dragons as cavalry for a program of racial extermination, is the most economically literate villain this reviewer has encountered in commercial fiction, which is not praise of the man but of the author’s eye. Consider the mechanism design. Dragons are ranked in periodic trials; winners enter the breeding stock, losers are castrated into the fighting stock. This is rank-order tournament theory administered with a literalism the incentive literature has so far declined to recommend.5 Rations are centrally allocated and promptly re-traded — two drakes swap an ore lump against a future cattle ration, with a negotiated commodity carve-out (“Agreed. Unless it is gold”), demonstrating that contingent contracting survives even in a barracks where the planners believe they have abolished exchange.

Shrewder still is the recruitment model. The movement’s founder was radicalized by dwarvish landlords taking “the lion’s share of each harvest”; his first disciple was a shipwright who read his own bankruptcy as an elvish conspiracy; his ablest engineer is a woman who joined because “he gives opportunities to those denied them elsewhere,” her barn designs having been credited to her brothers. The monster staffs itself from the talent the surrounding economy wastes — extractive land tenure, guild exclusion, and a glass ceiling are the recruiting sergeants, and grievance does the rest. That is the shrewdest observation in the book, and one of the shrewder ones in print anywhere.

Finally, the author permits his protagonist a finding most thrillers refuse: surveying the island, Auron concludes that the wizard “had constructed a system, a society, and one murder wouldn’t bring it down.” Institutions outlive their founders; assassination is not a development strategy. The climactic weapon, fittingly, is not the throat but the production function — the “great weakness of dragons” turns out to be that hatchlings imprint on whoever attends the egg, i.e., that preferences are endogenous and formed early, and the villain’s entire war machine is one long abuse of early-childhood investment. The epilogue’s hero, holding his own first clutch, responds not with a moral but with an institutional reform: separate the male hatchlings, teach the strong to protect the weak, and change the equilibrium in the nursery. The book ends, in other words, on human capital policy. I was not consulted, and it shows only slightly.

VII. Major Concerns

  1. The unit economics of dragon-hunting. The opening raid deploys a dwarvish column under arms, elvish cavalry, siege patience, specialized netting, and gas munitions, and sustains casualties. Realized revenue: one gray hatchling the buyer opens at “four hundred silver, elf,” plus a hoard the same dwarves dismiss on the spot (“Dragon hoard, indeed!”), plus a cross-claim for damages between the venture partners. The author quotes prices, which is commendable and rare; the prices condemn the business model. The industry is rescued off-page only by a downstream monopsonist paying “triple bounty for healthy female hatchlings” — and a sector kept solvent by one ideological buyer is not an industry, it is a bubble with a navy.
  2. The missing credit market. A continental caravan is provisioned, insured against steppe kings, and settled across two river systems without one syllable of interest, discounting, or bills of exchange. The money wagon only ever pays out. Capital, in this world, is apparently free. The author shares this belief with my undergraduates, and I extend to him the same annotation.
  3. Internal controls on the Isle of Ice. The villain runs history’s most capital-intensive military program — thirty-seven years of breeding stock projecting power with “eighteen pairs of wings” — and admits a walk-in applicant without searching his ears, in which the applicant is carrying a dwarvish hacksaw. Total collapse follows in roughly a week. As risk management this is indefensible; as history it is, I concede, the standard fate of extractive regimes, which are always audited last by the asset itself.
  4. Monetary hygiene. Coin in this economy is thrown into forests, retrieved by mouth, carried by mouth, counted, and spent on beef. Velocity: commendable. Sanitation: the reviewer declines to model it.

VIII. Minor Concerns

  1. The proof (ver. 0.7) offers “streneth,” “hatchline,” and “gapine jaws,” and appears to have been typeset by the copper hatchling, out of spite. The grade below is of the work; the proof receives its own line in Table 1.
  2. The hatchling lullaby runs: “‘Greed is good,’ or so foolish dragons will say / Until piles of treasure bring killing thieves where they lay.” The mother dragons of this book refute a famous financier in a bedtime song, treating the seven deadly sins as a portfolio of tail risks. My teaching evaluations request more of this and less of the midterm; the requests are related.
  3. Of the dwarves’ commerce the protagonist “could repeat back… the substance of what he had been taught, he didn’t really understand all the talk of supply and demand and rarity.” The most empirically realistic sentence in the volume. He is later given judicial and fiscal authority over a mountain range anyway, which is also empirically realistic.
  4. A dwarf defines the terms my colleagues confuse at every faculty meeting: “Honor is how others see you. Pride is how you see yourself.” Filed for administrative use.
  5. The units are a scandal: a “quiver” is the distance an archer walks collecting twelve arrows, and the protagonist counts thirty-seven horses in base eight, on his toes. The reviewer, who marks in base ten, envies him the material.
  6. The title undersells the thesis. Dragon Champion names the least interesting thing the protagonist does. The correct title appears at the head of this review, and the author may have it for the paperback, with attribution.

IX. Disposition

The stamp above did not exist before this review; it had to be carved for the occasion, and the carving was done slowly, in the hope the book would disappoint on a second reading. It did not. Dragon Champion is hereby entered on the ECON 101 optional reading list, effective the next printing of the syllabus, where it will sit beside Week 13 and outsell the textbook.

As for the student: the extra credit is awarded — one (1) point — subject to two conditions. First, the student will submit five hundred words on why a scaled dragon’s hoard, though made of coin, is not money — it is not held for exchange; it is working inventory of the scale industry; a wyrm’s M1 is measured in sheep. Second, the student will not inform the finance majors that the doctor spent his break reading a dragon novel, taking notes, and, at the passage where the dwarf explains trustworthiness, emitting what the glossary obliges me to record as a prrum.6

The scissors cut. The dragon eats the coin. The model clears. Accepted.
