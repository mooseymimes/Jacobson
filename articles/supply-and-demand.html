Article No. 005 · Public Instruction Division

The Scissors, Sharpened: Supply and Demand, Explained Once and For All

The department requested that Dr. Jacobson “make the material more accessible.” He has responded in the only acceptable manner: by taking up the chalk personally. What follows is the lecture as delivered — from memory, without notes, to sustained applause (attested: the janitor) — preserved on the first blackboard in the history of chalk to obey its lecturer.

Supply & Demand: The Complete Theory, in One Sitting

P. Jacobson, Dr. — chalk his own — erasure forbidden

I. The Law of Demand

As the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls. People buy less of what costs more.

Students find this law intuitive right up until they are asked to draw it, at which point the axes are swapped, the curve slopes upward, and someone writes “inverted Keynesian” in the margin. Note the convention: price on the vertical axis. We owe this to Marshall, and undergraduates protest it annually as though discovering injustice itself. The convention stands. So does the law.

II. The Law of Supply

As the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied rises. Sellers offer more of what pays more.

This is not greed; it is arithmetic wearing greed's coat. At a higher price, production that was yesterday a folly is today a business. The supply curve slopes upward for the same reason my office hours are fifteen minutes: scarce resources flow to their highest-valued use.

III. Equilibrium, or The Scissors

Which determines price — supply, or demand? The question is asked every September and deserves Marshall's answer: one may as reasonably ask which blade of a pair of scissors cuts the cloth. The curves cross; the market clears; the price at the crossing is P*, the quantity Q*. Above P*, unsold surplus embarrasses the sellers; below it, shortage embarrasses everyone. The market, unlike the student, corrects itself without being told twice.

IV. The Apparatus

Behold: the diagram, alive. No other blackboard has achieved this. Drag the chalk below to shift the curves and observe equilibrium recompute itself — instantly, obediently, and to seven decimal places, which is the minimum courtesy owed to posterity.

The board recomputes without complaint. Certain students could learn from it.

V. Common Errors of the Undergraduate Mind

  1. Confusing movement along a curve with a shift of the curve. A price change moves you along demand; it does not move demand. This is the cardinal sin, and it is committed hourly.
  2. Circling the intersection and calling it solved. The intersection is found, by algebra, not elected, by pen. Circling the biggest number is not a method in this course, nor — as the literature now records — in game theory.
  3. The non-binding price control. A price floor set below P* binds nothing except, eventually, the student's grade. The apparatus above will happily demonstrate; the desk-rejected manuscript of record demonstrates harder.

VI. Concluding Remark

Supply and demand are not opinions in tension; they are one machine seen from its two sides. The reader who has followed this lecture now understands price. The reader who has not is invited to read it again — the price of doing so has never been lower, and by the reader's own new theory, quantity demanded should respond accordingly.

Homework: Problem Set 1, ECON 101. Seven decimal places. You know why.
