The Anxiety of Impression: On Anne McCaffrey’s Dragonflight

McCaffrey, Anne. Dragonflight (The Dragonriders of Pern, Book One). Four parts, fourteen verse epigraphs (several recycled), one Dragondex. Reviewed from a scanned copy that wants its opening leaves, loses every italic, and misprints its own running head three ways (“Dragonfiight,” “Dragonftight,” “Dmgonfiight”) — a codex already halfway to legend, reviewed accordingly. Page references follow the scan’s running heads, where legible.

Reviewed by Paul Jacobson, Dr.

Department of Economics; and, effective this issue, the Department of English (annexed; the paperwork is a formality)

Gone away, gone ahead,
Echoes away, die unanswered.
Empty, open, dusty, dead,
Why have all the Weyrfolk fled? — the Question Song, four hundred Turns overdue for grading

Abstract—Following the Journal’s celebrated review of a dragon novel through the economic lens (No. 006), the Chair of the Department of English sent the reviewer a two-line note of congratulation so condescending it has been framed. In reprisal, the reviewer here reviews Anne McCaffrey’s Dragonflight with the English department’s own instruments — scansion, philology, narratology, close reading — wielded correctly for what appears to be the first time in that discipline’s history. The economic lens has been formally recused; contraband observations are impounded in a Sequestered Annex, struck through and unread. Findings: Dragonflight is a novel in which literary criticism is the load-bearing technology. Its curriculum is sung, its archives rot, its heroine defeats a planetary apocalypse by close-reading a quatrain, and its climactic act is the commissioning of a poem by its own future reader. The prose is frequently purple, the sexual politics require the frank paragraph they receive below, and the copy supplied is a disgrace that proves the book’s thesis. Emendations are supplied without royalty; the author’s understanding of her own book is graded (B+; the reviewer retains the curve). Impressed.

JEL codes: sealed by order of the reviewer (see Annex). Keywords: oral-formulaic transmission; hermeneutics; onomastics; anagnorisis; prosody; tense.

Editorial note. The Letters Division was voted into existence this morning, 2–1. The dissent (the reviewer, as Treasurer, citing scope) was overruled by the reviewer, as Editor-in-Chief, citing honor, and the reviewer, as incoming Chair of English, citing conquest. Terms of the recusal: for the duration of this review the reviewer will not model, price, graph, or optimize. Violations are seized at the border and deposited, unexamined, in the Annex following §II. Words marked † below were confiscated in transit.

I. Provenance of the Assignment

Two provocations arrived in the same week, which is efficient of my enemies.1 The first was the Chair’s note, quoted here in full because brevity should be punished: “Delighted to see Economics discover reading. Do tell us when you discover literature.” The second was my ECON 101 first-year — the Dragon Champion student, name still withheld pending the appeal they have now lost twice — sliding a second dragon novel across my desk with the words: “This one’s even more economics, Doctor. The entire plot is about tithe† compliance.”

The student is not wrong, which is what makes it a trap. Review this book as I reviewed the last and I hand the Chair her thesis: that an economist, given a poem, can only count it. I decline to be predictable twice at the same price. Accordingly I have done the one thing neither provocateur believed possible. I have read Dragonflight as literature, with the department’s own tools, to demonstrate that they were never difficult to operate — merely unsupervised. One reading sufficed to make me the foremost living authority on this novel, by any objective measure, particularly my own; the present essay is that authority, exercising itself. I will not count. I will scan.

II. The Work, Summarized for Colleagues

Begin where the book does: “Lessa woke, cold.” Three words, then the word cold twice more in the paragraph, an anaphora laid down like a drumbeat before we know a single fact. The heroine is introduced shoveling ashes from a hearth in the ruined Hold of her murdered family, disguised as a drudge, armed — the narrator tells us in a tricolon I intend to steal — with “perversity, endurance, and guile.” Colleagues, this is Cinderella: the ash-girl, the concealed birthright, the visiting prince (one F’lar, dragonman) on a formal bride-Search, and, in place of the slipper, a recognition scene in which the girl, scrubbed clean for the first time in ten years, fails to recognize her own face in a polished sheet of metal (p. 71). McCaffrey has taken the oldest tale in the European nursery and bolted it to a science-fictional chassis: the “Red Star” is an orbiting neighbor with a fifty-Turn ballistic timetable, the “Threads” are mindless space-borne spores, and the dragons are engineered fauna. The fairy tale is the fuselage; the orbital mechanics are the engine. It flies.

The plot thereafter: the usurper Fax is provoked into renouncing the Hold and dies on a dragonman’s dagger; Lessa is carried off to the last functioning Weyr, Impresses the queen dragon Ramoth, and discovers that the institution charged with defending the planet has dwindled to a shabby remnant, on strike against history† because the Holds no longer pay their tithe† and no one believes the old songs. The Red Star comes anyway. The Threads fall. And salvation arrives not by sword, not by science, but by a sequence of correctly performed acts of reading, catalogued in §III, culminating in the heroine riding a poem four hundred years into the past and towing five lost Weyrs home through time. Last year’s exhibit told us the economy has dragons in it. This year’s exhibit is stranger and finer: the library has wings.

III. The Central Contribution: A Heroine Who Reads

The thesis of this review, and I commend it to the seminar that will one day bear my name: Dragonflight is a novel about textual transmission in which every crisis is a crisis of interpretation, and every victory is a victory of close reading. The evidence is not subtle. It is the plumbing.

Consider the world’s information architecture. History survives in two failing media: skin Records that rot faster than they can be consulted, and Teaching Ballads drilled into children “letter-perfect” by rote. The heroine’s education consists of copying ballads ten times each onto a wax slate under R’gul, a tutor who has memorized the entire curriculum and understood none of it. And there, on pp. 86–90, McCaffrey stages — I can find no earlier instance in commercial fiction — a complete seminar in practical criticism. Lessa takes the quatrain “Seas boil and mountains move, / Sands heat, dragons prove / Red Star passes” and interrogates it line by line: “Dragons prove . . . Ambiguous for half a dozen interpretations, and R’gul won’t even suggest one as official. . . . Oh, all the things these ballads don’t say, and no one ever explains. Yet there must originally have been a reason.” That final sentence is the founding axiom of philology, arrived at by a teenager on a slate, and it does more work in this novel than every sword in it.

Her question — “Were not ballads constructed to inform? To teach those who could not read and write?” — is the oral-formulaic hypothesis entire, which I have here independently derived; I am told a Harvard man named Parry reached similar conclusions some decades earlier, an anticipation I am prepared to forgive.2 The verses are mnemonic machinery: weather data, defense doctrine, and orbital astronomy compressed into drum-tight tetrameter and handed down by people who no longer know they are carrying a manual. The novel’s villains, note, are not monsters but bad readers. Fax “taunts the harpers for the stupid nonsense of the old ballads and has banned from their repertoire all dragonlore” (p. 21) — canon suppression, performed on the page. R’gul proof-texts scripture he does not believe, intoning “Dragonman, avoid excess” to dress a surrender as a principle. The book’s great quarrel is fought entirely in quotation: “Don’t quote me verses I taught you as a weyrling,” R’gul snaps, and F’lar returns, “Then have faith in what you taught” (p. 146). Show me another adventure novel whose duels are exegetical.

Now the climax, which the Chair should read standing. The Masterharper Robinton exhumes a “Question Song” retired from the repertoire because it “unsettled singer as well as audience” — a riddle in a deliberately unresolved minor key, asking where five entire Weyrs of dragons went four hundred Turns ago. He observes, correctly, that a song this uncomfortable which refuses to die “was meant to be remembered. Those questions are important” (p. 234). Lessa then performs the single most consequential close reading in the genre’s history: “‘Gone away, gone . . . ahead!’ . . . All five Weyrs went ahead. But to when?” (p. 252). One stressed adverb, correctly weighed, converts an elegy into an itinerary. She mounts her dragon, uses a four-hundred-year-old tapestry as her reference image — dating it, as any manuscript scholar would, by what it omits: the door it depicts has no carved lintels, no Tower, no Court (“It is . . . and it isn’t,” p. 255) — and rides the interpretation into the past.

And there, at the far end of the jump, the novel closes its loop with a flourish I confess I did not anticipate: Lessa refuses to recite the Question Song to the Masterharper of the past. “You’ll write it, or your successor will. . . . it must be your doing, not my repeating” (p. 267). The poem exists because its future reader required it; the homework assigns itself; the deadline is four hundred years. Even the mechanism of travel is a compositional act — a dragon goes between only if its rider can hold a sufficiently vivid, sufficiently detailed image of the destination. Visualization, colleagues, is the fuel. McCaffrey has built the only faster-than-light drive in fiction that runs on description, and the only apocalypse averted by prosody. I have read the dissertations of an entire English faculty. None of them accomplishes as much with a quatrain. Whether the author knows the full extent of what she has built is examined in Table 1; establishing what an author has written was the English department’s job, and is therefore now mine.

IV. The Verse Apparatus, Scanned

Since the Chair believes I cannot scan, I scan. The opening epigraph:

Drummer, beat, and piper, blow,
Harper, strike, and soldier, go.
Free the flame and sear the grasses
Till the dawning Red Star passes.

DRUM-mer, BEAT, and PIP-er, BLOW

Trochaic tetrameter, catalectic in the first couplet — seven syllables, the final unstressed beat lopped off so the line lands on its feet like an order. This is the meter of drill and incantation (the witches in Macbeth use it; so do playground counting-rhymes, which are the same technology at different budgets), and it is exactly right for a curriculum meant to survive in the mouths of the illiterate. The drill-rhymes compress further as the content grows deadlier — “Wheel and turn / Or bleed and burn” is dimeter you could shout from dragonback in a crosswind — until doctrine reaches its terminal density in the gnomic triplet “Who wills, / Can. / Who tries, / Does. / Who loves, / Lives” (p. 262), which is barely language anymore; it is punctuation with convictions.3

Two structural observations the seminar should note. First, the epigraphs are a working repertoire, not decoration: several recur verbatim at different chapter heads, exactly as a living song-stock repeats itself, and the book’s first verse returns as its last. Second, and finer: at the first battle’s only human death, the verse migrates down from the epigraph into the story. C’gan, the Weyr’s old singer, dies on the sands quoting his own syllabus — “Too old. But ‘Dragonmen must fly / when Threads are in the sky’” (p. 207) — and the chapter closes with the narration itself taking up the couplet, as though the book were joining the chorus at his graveside. The first casualty of the war is the poet; his elegy is the curriculum; the Masterharper later performs the Question Song on the dead man’s guitar. If the Department of English has produced a tidier image of its own job description, it is keeping it from me.

V. Field Notes in Philology

A partial inventory of the language, offered in the spirit of the discipline I now administer:

  1. The honorific elision. A man who Impresses a dragon loses the middle of his name: the contraction (F’lar, F’nor, R’gul) is a morphological rank-badge, and the text plays its own name-game with it — a son is called T’kil because “T’lar might be confusing . . . ‘T’kil,’ however, still indicates sire as well as dam” (p. 155). Patronymic algebra, in dialogue. The women, meanwhile, keep every vowel they were born with: on Pern the apostrophe is a gendered morpheme, and someone should set the question in an exam.
  2. The dragons in -th. Mnementh, Ramoth, Canth, Nemorth, Piyanth — and one beast named, simply, Hath: the King James third-person singular of to have, with wings. As for the bronze Mnementh, the Greek mnēmē, memory, sits inside his name like a rider: the most faithful mind in a book about forgetting is called, in effect, the Rememberer. Whether the author intended this I decline to investigate, since every possible answer diminishes someone.4
  3. The lexicon as archaeology. Years are “Turns” — a calendar that has forgotten everything but rotation, which is what a calendar looks like after history burns down. The nightbeast is a “watch-wher,” and the nursery rhyme puns the creature into its own function: “Watch-wher, watch-wher, / In your lair, / Watch well, watch-wher! / Who goes there?” Best of all, the smiths’ corrosive is called agenothree — say it aloud, colleagues: HNO3, nitric acid, a chemical formula fossilized into a word by four hundred years of mouths that kept the sound and lost the science. One morpheme of dramatic irony: the reader decodes what the speaker cannot.
  4. The oath inventory. “By the Egg”; “By the shell of the first Egg”; “By the golden shell of the queen”; “By the Void that spawned us”; “under the double moons.” Profanity is the cheapest worldbuilding there is and among the most load-bearing: a culture confesses its cosmology every time it stubs a toe. Pern swears by eggs. Pern is correct to.
  5. Syntax under time travel. Pausing mid-jump above a year she already occupies twice, Lessa reports: “I’m too many in this morning” (p. 271). Grammatical number, broken cleanly across the knee of the plot. It is the best sentence in the book and there is no verse in it; I note for the record that it took a time machine to make English do that.
  6. Reported dragon. The dragons, the most intimate voices in the novel, are never granted quotation marks; they arrive entirely in reported speech, a running translation from a language we are never shown. The device pays for itself in comedy alone — “Mnementh informed F’lar that he, a fully matured bronze dragon, was no relation to any scrawny, crawling, chained, and wing-clipped watch-wher” (p. 64) — and in one line of perfect deadpan the queen files with posterity: “That is, if Mnementh can catch me, Ramoth amended smugly” (p. 197). Indirect discourse as characterization: the seminar may begin there.

VI. The Arrhenius Plate; or, How Not to Read

Every discipline should be made to watch its own nightmare performed as comedy, and McCaffrey obliges mine — my new one, I mean. Midway through the book (p. 179), the protagonists find a metal plate from the founders’ era, imperishable where every skin Record rots. It reads, in part:

“Mother’s father’s father, who departed for all time between, said this was the key to the mystery, and it came to him while doodling: he said that he said: ARRHENIUS? EUREKA! MYCORRHIZA. . . . Flamethrowing fire lizards to wipe out the spores. Q.E.D.” — the plate, nailed to a Record chest, surviving all its readers

Here is the founding science of their entire predicament — a panspermia hypothesis, a fungal analogy, a proof concluded — in their hands, in permanent material, and watch the hermeneutics deployed against it. F’lar, confidently: “The only way to depart for all time between is to die, right? . . . So it is a death vision, dutifully recorded by a grandchild, who couldn’t spell very well either. ‘Doodling’ as the present tense of dying!” Lessa, gamely trying the other genre on for size: “A badly scored ballad?” Two of the sharpest minds on the planet, a primary source, and an interpretive apparatus pointed with total confidence in the wrong direction. The scene is the inverse of the Question Song — reading as catastrophe averted there, reading as catastrophe rehearsed here — and together they are the book’s whole argument: a civilization’s fate is decided by the quality of its philology. I have sat in faculty meetings that were this scene with worse catering.

Against these failures the novel sets its one professional: Robinton, Masterharper, the book’s true critic and its best character by the length of the continent. He reads score-deep (“I know, to the last full stop in the score, your opinion of dragonkind”), he deploys satire as a siege weapon — his one-line spoken review, “Truly all shall pay heed to the master,” carries a barb the room takes a full beat to feel — and at the Council he performs ventriloquized parodies of two Lords to their faces and is thanked for it. His guild, mocked in prosperity, beaten “for singing the old songs as is their duty,” turns out to have been carrying the planet’s survival manual through four hundred years of budget cuts. The English department of Pern is called the Harpercraft, and McCaffrey’s treatment of it is the most flattering portrait my new department has ever received: ignored right up until the sky begins to fall, then indispensable. I would resent the compliment less if it were less accurate.5

VII. Major Concerns

  1. The sexual politics. The seminar will need this paragraph, so let it be exact. F’lar shakes Lessa when she defies him — I count six administered shakings, one promised (“He will shake me and shake me. He always shakes me when I disobey him,” p. 263), and one visibly suppressed at the reunion — and of the consummation under the dragons’ mating flight the narration itself concedes, flatly, that absent the dragon-bond “he might as well call it rape” (p. 152). The sentence is the text’s own; 1968 wrote its assumptions in plain sight where its descendants learned to euphemize. That frankness is evidence, not absolution: the novel builds the most formidable woman in its genre’s memory and then grades her rebellions as tantrums to be shaken out. Read it whole, teach it honestly, and let the couplet “Who loves, / Lives” sit uneasily beside p. 152, where the book put it.
  2. The welds show. The volume is a fix-up of two award-taking novellas with connective tissue, and the seams are load-bearing: Part I closes on the Impression at full lyric pressure; Part II reopens with a lecture-hall recap conducted by the two dullest men on Pern. The recycled epigraphs, which I praised above as repertoire, are also, let us admit, inventory stretched across a gap. A great book assembled from parts is still assembled.
  3. The adverbial school of dialogue. Speeches here are said tartly, acidly, archly, sweetly, “with caustic sweetness,” and “with a touch of her usual asperity,” frequently within a single audience with the Weyrwoman. The tags are emotional stage directions for a cast the author does not quite trust to act. When she does trust them — the R’gul quotation duel, every line Robinton speaks — the adverbs vanish and the scene stands up on its own. The lesson was available in-house.
  4. Registral whiplash. A narration frank enough to print p. 152 nevertheless dissolves every consummation into asterisks and firelight, and its ecstasies run purple (“a tidal wave rising relentlessly from the sea of her soul”). The book cannot decide whether it is candid or courtly and so alternates, sometimes within a page. I dock it accordingly and note that the alternation itself dates the volume as surely as the tapestry’s missing lintels.
  5. The pistol on the mantel, left to rust. Having staged the great misreading of §VI, the author sets the Arrhenius plate down and the novel never picks it up again: the founders’ own answer to the Threads exits the book unread by anyone but this reviewer. A pistol is hung upon the mantel in the second act, and the curtain falls on it rusting. I am informed that later volumes discharge it. That is a cheque this volume writes and declines to cash, and I mark the volume I was given; serial absolution is a courtesy for serial readers, and the foremost authority reads one book at a time, completely.

VIII. Minor Concerns

  1. The condition of the copy. My scan wants its opening leaves (colleagues assure me editions exist with a formal overture; mine begins mid-legend, which is thematically correct and bibliographically scandalous), renders the hero variously as F’lar, Flar, P’lar, and Plar, prints the planet as “Pem” for pages at a stretch, and offers “the napping of Ramoth’s immense wings” where the queen is beating them. In any other book this would be mere vandalism. In this one it is a peer-reviewed demonstration: the text about texts decaying into legend is itself decaying into legend, and I have marked the scan’s errors as the early blossoms of a future Question Song.6
  2. The author reviews herself. Lessa, of the rotting skin Records: “We have become, dear Weyrleader, entirely too hidebound” — a pun on the storage medium, at which F’lar “roared with appreciation” (p. 176). An author who plants a pun and then applauds it on the next line has performed autopeer-review. Verdict sustained; the pun is good; the claque is noted.
  3. The fate of the literalist. R’gul, having taught the verses all his life without believing a word, beholds the returned five Weyrs and — the book’s driest sentence — “got quietly drunk” (p. 279). Filed beside certain colleagues’ reactions to this Division’s founding.
  4. The Dragondex. The volume closes with an index of invented things, the apparatus of scholarship annexed by fiction. As one annexer to another: respect.
  5. The sequel, planted in plain sight. On the final page F’lar shakes his fist at the Red Star and vows, “We will fall on you, where you spin, and sear you on your own ground.” The author has left a hook in the last paragraph the way her balladeers leave clues in a chorus: conspicuously, for readers four hundred Turns — or one paperback — ahead.

IX. Emendations, Offered Without Royalty

Last issue this Journal handed an author a better title for his paperback, with attribution. The Letters Division continues the charity, for the foremost authority on a book owes its author his marginalia. Four corrections, gratis:

  1. p. 124, the ecstasy. “A tidal wave rising relentlessly from the sea of her soul flooded Lessa.” Strike; substitute “Desire took her.” Eleven words are returned to the author for use elsewhere in the series. No charge.
  2. p. 234, the rhyme. The Question Song’s third stanza rhymes Weyr with Weyr, an identical rhyme I return to undergraduates with a circle around it and one raised eyebrow, renewable. In the balladeer’s defense, hammering the keyword four times into four centuries of memory was the actual assignment, and craft bows to payload. Marked wrong; graded right; the eyebrow stands.
  3. The adverbs. Tartly, acidly, archly, and sweetly are dismissed with severance; an invoice for the surplus accompanies this offprint. The dialogue stood unassisted for three pages of the R’gul duel and can be taught to do so everywhere. My door is open; my rates are framed beside it.
  4. Part II, the recap. The lecture-hall exposition conducted by R’gul and S’lel compresses, in my edition, to a single paragraph. The reader who requires more R’gul has never met one.

The Division required its own stamp; ACCEPT belongs to Economics and shall not be loaned. The word was chosen with the full weight of the pun: on Pern, Impression is the irreversible bond formed at first meeting between a creature and the one who will carry it thereafter; in the printing house, a first impression is the earliest state of a text; and in the reviewer’s office it is what occurred, involuntarily, at p. 252, when the adverb turned and the Weyrs went ahead. Dragonflight is hereby entered into the Jacobson Canon of Letters as its founding text — a canon whose complete current contents it is — and cross-listed nowhere. It does not join the ECON 101 optional reading list. It is too good for the company.

Dispositions of the parties. The student: the tithe thesis is desk-rejected as a replication study — the Journal established last issue that dragon novels contain economics, and I do not fund confirmations. Extra credit: one (1) point, denominated in Letters-Division units, which convert to ECON 101 course credit at a rate the recusal forbids me to compute. The point becomes negotiable upon submission of five hundred words on why the Question Song is the only homework assignment in literature that grades its own professor, deadline four hundred Turns or Friday, whichever the student reaches first. The Chair: she will receive this offprint by campus mail, with the annexation instrument and a note reading, in full: “Discovered. It was where you left it.”

The harper strikes. The reader rides. The text arrives on time, four hundred years after it was assigned. Impressed.
